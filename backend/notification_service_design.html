01 — One event, many independent deliveries

Fan-out delivery patterns

Start here, before any code. Imagine you're the host of a dinner party and you need to tell three separate people the venue changed: you could walk over, personally tell person A, wait until that conversation fully wraps up, then walk to person B, wait for that to finish, then walk to person C — if person B is hard to track down, persons A and C's news is fine, but YOU are stuck waiting on B before you can even start on C. Or, you could pin one note to a shared board and let each person check the board on their own time, independently, at their own pace — you're done the instant the note is pinned, regardless of how long it takes any individual person to actually notice it.

A fan-out is exactly that shared board: one logical event (e.g. "order shipped") needs to reach MANY separate subscribers — an email handler, a push notification handler, an SMS handler, maybe an in-app notification handler too. The question is how the one event becomes those many independent delivery attempts.

Naive synchronous fan-out

The naive approach: the event producer (the order service, say) directly and synchronously calls each handler, one after another, inside the same request. Call the email handler, wait for it to finish. Call the push handler, wait for it to finish. Call the SMS handler, wait for it to finish. Only then does the producer's request actually complete. If the SMS provider is slow or down, the email handler — which would have succeeded instantly — is stuck waiting behind it, and the producer's original HTTP request stays open the entire time, for as long as the slowest, flakiest channel takes.

Async, queue-based fan-out

The queue-based approach: the event is published once to a message broker (e.g. Kafka, SQS, RabbitMQ), and each channel handler independently subscribes to that stream and pulls its own copy whenever it's ready. The producer's job ends the moment the event is published — it doesn't wait for any handler, doesn't even know how many handlers exist. Each handler processes at its own pace, fully decoupled from the others and from the original producer. A slow SMS provider only slows down SMS delivery; email and push are entirely unaffected.

Why this matters in practice: a synchronous fan-out means your order-confirmation API's response time is only as fast as your slowest notification channel — a flaky third-party SMS vendor now determines how long every customer waits for "your order was placed" to return, even though SMS was never the critical part of that response.
Interview-ready definition: fan-out is the pattern of turning one logical event into multiple independent downstream delivery attempts. Synchronous fan-out has the producer call each handler directly and sequentially, coupling the producer's latency and availability to every handler's latency and availability. Queue-based (asynchronous) fan-out has the producer publish the event once to a broker, after which each channel handler subscribes and processes independently — decoupling failure and latency in any one channel from every other channel and from the original producer.
02 — What happens when a delivery attempt fails

Retry with exponential backoff, and the dead-letter queue

Start here, before the demo. Imagine calling a friend who isn't picking up. You don't hang up and never try again (you'd miss them for something urgent) — but you also don't call back instantly, over and over, every second (that's just annoying and unlikely to help, since whatever kept them from answering the first time probably hasn't changed in one second). Instead you wait a bit, try again, and if that fails too, you wait even longer before the next attempt — giving whatever's wrong on their end more time to resolve. And if you've called five times over an hour with no luck, you stop calling and instead leave a voicemail for a human to deal with later, rather than either calling forever or silently giving up and never mentioning it to anyone.

That's exactly the shape of production-grade retry handling. When a specific delivery attempt fails (the email API times out, the push provider returns a 500, the SMS gateway rejects the number), the system doesn't give up immediately, but it also doesn't retry instantly in a tight loop. It waits, retries, and if that fails again, waits longer before the next attempt — exponential backoff: attempt 1 fails, wait ~1s, attempt 2 fails, wait ~2s, attempt 3 fails, wait ~4s, and so on, roughly doubling each time. This spaces out retries so a transient blip (a momentary network hiccup, a provider briefly overloaded) has time to actually clear before the next attempt, instead of hammering an already-struggling provider with a tight retry loop that makes things worse.

Why retrying forever is not the answer

After a configured maximum number of attempts (commonly 3-5), retrying stops. Some failures are permanent, not transient — a malformed payload that will never parse correctly, a recipient email address or phone number that's permanently invalid, an account that's been deleted. No amount of waiting and retrying fixes a permanent failure; it's not a timing problem, it's a data problem. Retrying a permanently-broken message forever would mean the retry worker keeps burning cycles on a message that can never succeed, and — depending on how the retry queue is structured — could even block healthy messages behind it from ever getting processed.

Why a dead-letter queue exists (not "just drop it")

The alternative to retrying forever isn't "silently drop the message" either. A dead-letter queue (DLQ) is a separate queue or store where a message goes after it exhausts its retry budget. This exists to solve two problems at once:

Problem avoidedHow the DLQ avoids it
Retrying foreverThe message stops consuming retry-worker capacity once it's moved to the DLQ — the worker is now free to process other, healthy messages instead of looping on one that can never succeed
Silently dropping the messageThe message is preserved intact (full payload, failure history) in the DLQ, not deleted — so a human can inspect it later, understand why it failed, fix the root cause, and potentially reprocess it manually

# Exponential backoff retry loop, ending in a dead-letter queue async def deliver_with_retry(message, max_attempts=5): for attempt in range(1, max_attempts + 1): try: await send_to_provider(message) return # success -- done, nothing else to do except DeliveryError: if attempt == max_attempts: await move_to_dead_letter_queue(message, attempt) return wait_seconds = 2 ** (attempt - 1) # 1, 2, 4, 8, ... await sleep(wait_seconds) # attempt 1 fails -> wait 1s -> attempt 2 fails -> wait 2s -> attempt 3 # fails -> wait 4s -> attempt 4 fails -> wait 8s -> attempt 5 fails -> DLQ
Interview-ready definition: exponential backoff retries a failed operation after progressively longer waits (roughly doubling each time) rather than immediately or at a fixed interval, giving transient failures room to clear without hammering an already-struggling downstream provider. A dead-letter queue is where a message goes after exhausting its retry budget — it exists specifically so a permanently-failing message neither retries forever (starving the worker of capacity to process healthy messages) nor gets silently dropped (losing the message and the ability to debug why it failed); instead it's preserved intact in a separate location for human inspection and possible manual reprocessing once the root cause is fixed.
03 — The same idea, three genuinely different deliveries

Multi-channel delivery

Start here, before the examples. Telling someone "your order shipped" in an email, a push notification, and a text message isn't like photocopying the same note three times. It's more like telling the same news to three different audiences who each need it in a different format: a formal letter with full details and a signature block, a one-line note stuck on the fridge, and a quick text to someone who's mid-commute and can only glance at their phone for two seconds. Same underlying news — three genuinely different presentations, lengths, and delivery mechanisms.

The same logical notification ("your order has shipped") needs its own template, its own transport protocol, its own size constraints, and often its own separate vendor integration per channel. It is never just "the same string sent to three different APIs."

Email

Full HTML template, subject line, unsubscribe footer (often legally required). Sent via SMTP or an email API (SendGrid, SES). Failure modes: bounces, spam-filtering, provider rate limits.

Push

Short title + body, plus a structured deep-link payload so tapping it opens the right screen. Sent via APNs (iOS) or FCM (Android). Failure modes: expired device tokens, app uninstalled, payload size limits (a few KB).

SMS

Plain text only, hard character limit (160 chars per segment). Sent via a telephony API like Twilio. Failure modes: invalid/disconnected numbers, carrier filtering, per-message cost.

The trap: treating multi-channel delivery as "format the string once, send it everywhere" breaks the moment any channel has a real constraint — an HTML email body sent raw as an SMS blows past the 160-character limit instantly, and a push notification payload built from a full HTML email template blows past APNs/FCM's payload size cap and gets silently rejected by the provider.
Interview-ready definition: multi-channel delivery means rendering one logical notification into a channel-specific template — a different format, transport protocol, size constraint, and often a different vendor integration per channel — rather than broadcasting one identical payload everywhere. Each channel also has its own distinct failure modes (bounces for email, expired tokens for push, invalid numbers for SMS), so a robust notification service tracks delivery success/failure per channel independently, not as one aggregate "did the notification go out" boolean.

Problem 1 · Designing fan-out for a new event type

A product team adds a new event type, "payment.failed", which needs to notify the user by email AND trigger an internal Slack alert to the billing team. Sketch how this should fan out, and explain what breaks if it's implemented as a synchronous chain of calls inside the payment service's request handler.

Show solution
The payment service should publish one "payment.failed" event to the message broker and be done -- it should not know or care that there are two downstream consumers. Two independent subscribers then each pull their own copy: a user-notification handler (sends the email) and an internal-alerting handler (posts to Slack). Adding a third consumer later (e.g. a fraud-detection handler) requires zero changes to the payment service itself, since it only ever publishes to the broker. If implemented as a synchronous chain instead -- payment service calls the email handler, waits, then calls the Slack-alert handler, waits -- two problems appear. First, the payment service's request (which the user is waiting on, likely to see a "payment failed" error) now blocks on both the email provider AND Slack's API being fast and available, neither of which the user should ever have to wait on. Second, if Slack's API is down, does the whole payment-failure flow fail? If the code isn't carefully written to swallow that error, an unrelated internal alerting outage could actually crash or hang the user-facing payment failure response itself.
Problem 2 · Calculating total backoff delay across N retries

Using exponential backoff starting at 1 second and doubling each attempt (1s, 2s, 4s, 8s, ...), with a max of 5 attempts before moving to the DLQ, what is the total elapsed wall-clock time spent waiting (not counting the delivery attempts themselves) between attempt 1 and the message landing in the DLQ?

Show solution
15 seconds total wait time. With 5 attempts, there are 4 waits between them (a wait only happens between a failed attempt and the next retry -- there's no wait needed after the final attempt, since it goes straight to the DLQ): wait after attempt 1: 1s wait after attempt 2: 2s wait after attempt 3: 4s wait after attempt 4: 8s attempt 5 fails -> immediately moved to DLQ, no further wait Total = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15 seconds. In general, with backoff starting at B seconds and doubling, N attempts produce (N-1) waits summing to B * (2^(N-1) - 1) seconds -- here B=1, N=5, giving 1 * (2^4 - 1) = 15.
Problem 3 · Why a DLQ isn't the same as dropping the message

A teammate argues: "once we've retried 5 times and it still fails, the message is basically unrecoverable anyway -- why not just log an error and discard it, instead of building a whole separate dead-letter queue?" Explain precisely what's lost by discarding instead of routing to a DLQ.

Show solution
A log line saying "delivery failed" is not the same as having the actual message. Discarding the message loses the full payload, the recipient details, the failure history (which provider, which error, on which attempt), and any context needed to actually fix the underlying problem and RE-deliver it. A log entry tells you something failed; a DLQ entry gives you everything needed to act on it. Concretely: suppose the root cause turns out to be a bug in how phone numbers were formatted before being sent to the SMS provider (a code bug, not a genuinely bad number) -- once that bug is fixed, every message sitting in the DLQ can be reprocessed and successfully delivered late, rather than lost. If those messages had been discarded instead, that recovery is simply impossible -- the actual message content no longer exists anywhere, and every affected user silently never received their notification, with no way to even know how many were affected or who they were.
Problem 4 · When the retry queue itself backs up

Suppose a downstream email provider goes fully down for 30 minutes. During that window, every email delivery attempt fails and gets scheduled for retry with backoff. What happens to the retry queue during and after that outage, and what design choices make this scenario better or worse?

Show solution
During the outage, every email send fails on attempt 1, gets scheduled to retry after ~1s, fails again, retries after ~2s, and so on -- but since the provider is down for the FULL 30 minutes, essentially every message in flight during that window keeps failing and re-queuing with growing backoff, and meanwhile brand new "order shipped" events keep arriving and joining the same retry queue. The retry queue's size grows for the whole 30 minutes, and once the provider recovers, there's a large backlog of delayed retries plus fresh messages all competing for processing capacity at once -- a "thundering herd" of retries hitting the just-recovered provider simultaneously, which can even cause a second, self-inflicted outage if the provider is still fragile. Design choices that help: (1) capping the max retry queue depth or age so old messages don't retry indefinitely into an already-degraded system, (2) adding jitter (small random variation) to backoff delays so retries don't all fire in lockstep and slam the recovering provider at the exact same instant, (3) keeping retry queues PER CHANNEL so an email-provider outage's backlog doesn't consume processing capacity that push and SMS delivery need, and (4) circuit-breaking -- if a provider is failing consistently, stop attempting new sends to it for a cooldown period instead of queuing more doomed attempts, then probe periodically to see if it has recovered.
Problem 5 · Synchronous vs queue-based fan-out for two different scenarios

Consider two notification scenarios: (a) a payment confirmation that must reliably reach the user, where losing it silently would be a real problem, and (b) a low-priority "someone liked your post" notification, where an occasional missed delivery is a minor, acceptable annoyance. Which fan-out approach fits each, and why?

Show solution
Both scenarios are actually better served by QUEUE-BASED fan-out, but for different reasons, and the "must reliably reach the user" one leans on it much more heavily. For the payment confirmation (a): reliability is the priority, and a message broker gives durability guarantees a synchronous call doesn't -- once the event is published, it persists in the queue even if the notification service crashes immediately after, and can be retried with backoff and ultimately land in a DLQ for manual follow-up rather than silently vanishing because one synchronous call happened to fail with no retry logic wrapped around it. A synchronous approach here risks the payment service's own response failing or hanging if the notification call errors out or is slow, entangling an unrelated notification concern with the core payment flow's reliability. For the "someone liked your post" notification (b): queue-based fan-out is still the right default (it's simply the better default architecture for decoupling reasons -- keeping the producer fast regardless of channel health), but the RELIABILITY bar around it can be much lower: fewer retry attempts, a shorter or skipped backoff, and possibly no DLQ at all (or a DLQ that's periodically bulk-discarded rather than manually inspected), since an occasional missed "like" notification has low cost. The architectural pattern (publish once, consume independently) is the same in both cases -- what differs is how much retry/DLQ rigor is worth building around it, tuned to how costly a missed delivery actually is.
