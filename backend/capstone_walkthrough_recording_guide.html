01 — Five beats, in a fixed order, that scale to any time budget

A suggested narrative structure

Start here, before opening a recording app. Think about the difference between someone giving you turn-by-turn directions versus someone handing you a map and letting you find your own way. A rambling, chronological account of "first I built the scaffold, then I added the orchestrator, then..." is turn-by-turn directions — technically complete, but the listener has to hold the whole thing in their head to see the shape. A structured walkthrough is the map: problem, then architecture, then decisions, then trade-offs, then what's next — a fixed order that lets a listener orient themselves at any point, and that you can compress or expand to fit any time budget without losing coherence.

02 — Why recording yourself beats rehearsing silently

Practicing out loud and recording yourself

Start here, before deciding this step is unnecessary. Think about the difference between reading sheet music in your head and actually playing the piece out loud. Reading silently, every passage feels fluent — your inner voice never stumbles, never runs out of breath, never loses the thread mid-phrase. Playing it out loud reveals the ACTUAL friction points: the phrase that's harder to say than it was to think, the transition that doesn't flow, the section where you realize you don't actually know the next word. A verbal walkthrough has the exact same gap between "I understand this" and "I can say this smoothly, cold, under time pressure" — and the only way to find that gap is to actually talk, not think.

Practice levelWhat it catchesWhat it misses
Silent mental rehearsalWhether you know the FACTSWhether you can PRODUCE them fluently under time pressure — the actual skill being tested
Talking out loud, unrecordedVerbal stumbles, pacing awareness in the momentAnything you don't notice in real time — most people underestimate their own filler words and pacing problems while speaking
Recorded and played backEverything above, PLUS an honest outside view of pacing, jargon, and whether you're actually answering "why" — the only method that catches what you can't self-monitor while talkingNothing structural — this is the complete method, just costs more time per rep
How to actually record this, practically: a phone voice memo is enough — video adds value (catching filler gestures, eye contact if presenting to a screen) but audio alone already catches most of what matters: pacing, filler words, and whether each beat from section 1 actually gets said in the order and depth intended. Do the FULL five-beat walkthrough in one uninterrupted take at your target time budget, exactly like dsa/capstone_combined_mock_full_loop.html's own "run it as one continuous loop, not paused and resumed" rule for mocks — a walkthrough recorded in disconnected fragments doesn't reveal the same pacing and transition problems a real, continuous delivery does.
03 — Listen back once for each of these, not all at once

Self-critique checklist

Start here, before your first playback. Trying to catch pacing, jargon, AND "why vs. what" all in one listen splits attention across three different kinds of listening — each one is a genuinely different skill to check for. Practice problems

Quick-fire drill: a real walkthrough almost always gets interrupted with a follow-up mid-flow. Practice answering each of these in under 60 seconds, out loud, before revealing what a strong answer covers.

Problem 1 · "You said Redis for run state — what happens if Redis goes down mid-run?"

Answer in under 60 seconds, out loud, before revealing.

Show what a strong 60-second answer covers
A strong answer names the CONCRETE failure mode, not a vague "it would be bad": any run with in-flight state loses that state -- the run's turns list and status become unrecoverable, since Redis is the ONLY place that ephemeral state lives (capstone_orchestrator_integration .html section 1's explicit trade-off: Redis's weaker durability guarantee was accepted BECAUSE the state is disposable). Concretely: a client mid-conversation would get an error or a fresh, empty run on their next request, since the orchestrator has no memory of the prior turns. What DOESN'T break: anything already durably recorded -- agent_run, tool_call, eval_span, eval_result in Postgres are unaffected, since they're written to a completely separate store with its own durability guarantees. So a Redis outage loses IN-FLIGHT work, not HISTORICAL record -- a meaningful distinction worth stating explicitly rather than treating "Redis is down" as one uniform bad outcome. What you'd do about it, if pushed further: Redis persistence options (RDB snapshots or AOF) trade some write performance for surviving a restart, or, more robustly, treating an in-flight run as always resumable by re-deriving state from tool_call and eval_span's already-durable partial record rather than depending on Redis as the only source -- a real design change worth naming as a "future work" item rather than something the schema already handles.
Problem 2 · "If you had one more week, what would you actually build next?"

Answer in under 60 seconds, out loud, before revealing.

Show what a strong 60-second answer covers
The weak version of this answer is a vague wishlist ("more features, better UI, more testing"). The strong version names ONE specific thing, grounded in a REAL finding from this week's actual work, with a concrete reason it's the highest-priority next step over other options. A strong worked example: "The load test found the orchestrator's Redis lock becomes the binding constraint above roughly 140 concurrent users. I'd implement the atomic-operation fix from capstone_ orchestrator_integration.html's own practice problem -- restructuring state as a Redis-native RPUSH instead of load-modify-save -- since that's the single highest-leverage change: it directly addresses the ACTUAL measured bottleneck rather than a guessed one, and load_ testing.html's own data shows exactly where the current ceiling is." What makes this strong: it's SPECIFIC (not "improve performance" but the exact fix, from the exact lab, addressing the exact measured number), it's PRIORITIZED with a stated reason (highest-leverage because it's the MEASURED bottleneck, not a guess), and it demonstrates that "what's next" flows directly from real testing done this week rather than being an unrelated wishlist item disconnected from the actual system's demonstrated weak points.
Self-check before recording your real take: could you, without notes, (1) name all five beats of the narrative structure in order, (2) state which specific finding from this week you'd use for beat 4 (limitations), and (3) answer both quick-fire questions above in under 60 seconds each, cold?
