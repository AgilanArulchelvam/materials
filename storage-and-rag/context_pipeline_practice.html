Problem 1 · Explaining why the distractor documents didn't win retrieval

Both return-process.md and damaged-item-policy.md mention refunds and timeframes. Using the hybrid_search and re-ranking reasoning from earlier this month, explain why damaged-item-policy.md should score higher for this specific question, not just "because it's the right answer."

Show solution
The question is specifically "options for a lamp that arrived DAMAGED" -- the word "damaged" and the underlying concept it invokes (an item defective on arrival, not a voluntary return of an undamaged item) are the key discriminating signal. damaged-item-policy.md's content is built directly around that concept ("items arriving damaged," "photos of the damage," "no return shipping required"), giving it strong semantic and lexical overlap with the question on the SPECIFIC dimension that matters here. return-process.md, by contrast, describes the process for returning a "non-damaged item" -- it shares surface-level vocabulary with the question (refunds, timeframes, order history) but is actually describing the opposite scenario. A retrieval system relying purely on loose keyword overlap without discriminating on the actual distinguishing concept ("damaged" vs "non-damaged") could plausibly rank these two documents closer together than they should be -- this is exactly why this lab's setup calls return-process.md a "distractor": it's the kind of document a weaker retrieval/ranking approach might over-rank, while a good embedding model (capturing that "damaged" and "non-damaged" are meaningfully different states, not just similar words) or a re-ranking pass (directly comparing the query against each candidate) should correctly separate them.
Problem 2 · Deciding what to compress if the conversation history were much longer

Suppose the conversation history in section 1 were 40 turns long instead of 5, with the damaged-lamp discussion only appearing in the LAST 5 turns (the first 35 turns were about a completely unrelated earlier order). Using the prompt_compression lab's reasoning, what should stage 2 of this pipeline do, and what's the risk if it compressed indiscriminately?

Show solution
Stage 2 should compress the first 35 (unrelated, older) turns into a short summary while keeping the last 5 turns -- the ones actually about the current damaged-lamp question -- verbatim. This is exactly the prompt_compression lab's core pattern: fold the OLDER, now-less-relevant portion into a gist-preserving summary, and keep the RECENT, clearly- still-relevant portion untouched, rather than treating the entire history as a single undifferentiated block to compress or keep as one unit. The risk of compressing indiscriminately (e.g. summarizing ALL 40 turns uniformly, including the most recent 5): the specific details from the CURRENT damaged-lamp conversation -- the order number, the fact photos were being attached, the specific question just asked -- could get flattened into the same lossy gist-level summary the prompt_compression lab warned about, exactly the "a specific number or exact phrasing becomes unrecoverable" failure mode from that lab, except now affecting the MOST relevant, most recently-discussed content rather than genuinely old material. This is precisely why compression needs to be applied selectively (older turns) rather than uniformly across the whole history -- uniform compression would treat "5 turns ago, still relevant" the same as "35 turns ago, no longer relevant," which the keep_recent parameter from the prompt_compression lab exists specifically to avoid.
Problem 3 · Reordering the assembled prompt and predicting the effect

Stage 3 assembles the prompt with retrieved context placed right before the question (context_assembly's recommended ordering). Suppose instead the pipeline placed retrieved context FIRST, before the conversation history and the question. Using the context_assembly lab's recency-effect reasoning, what's the concrete risk for THIS specific example?

Show solution
Per the context_assembly lab, content positioned closer to the end of the prompt (closer to where generation begins) tends to receive stronger attention from many models than content buried earlier. If the damaged-item-policy.md chunk were moved to the very front of the prompt -- ahead of the conversation history AND the question -- it would be sitting furthest from the position the model tends to weight most heavily, right as the model is about to generate its answer. The concrete risk for this example: the model might underweight the actual policy details (refund/replacement within 30 days, no return shipping required, photo requirement) relative to the conversation history and question that now sit closer to the generation point -- increasing the chance of a vaguer, less policy-grounded answer (e.g. generically suggesting "please contact support" instead of citing the specific 30-day/no-return-shipping/photo-based policy the retrieved chunk actually contains), even though the correct chunk WAS technically included in the prompt. This mirrors the context_assembly lab's broader point directly: chunk SELECTION (having the right document in context at all) and chunk POSITIONING (where in the prompt it sits) are separate concerns, and getting selection right doesn't guarantee the model actually leans on it if positioning works against it.
Problem 4 · Tracing what happens if retrieval's top document had a low similarity score

Suppose this corpus didn't actually contain any document about damaged items at all — the closest match, shipping-times.md, only scores 0.31 similarity against the question. Using the rag_qa_system_design lab's low-confidence-retrieval reasoning, what should stage 1 (or a check right after it) do differently in this version of the scenario?

Show solution
This is exactly the low-confidence-retrieval scenario from the rag_qa_system_design lab: even though retrieval always returns SOME top-scoring result (shipping-times.md at 0.31 here), a genuinely low top score is a real signal that nothing actually relevant exists in the corpus for this question, not evidence that shipping-times.md is actually a decent answer. If stage 1 simply proceeds to stages 2 and 3 with this weak match, stage 3 would assemble a prompt built around a document that doesn't actually address the question at all, and stage 4 generation would risk producing a plausible-sounding but ungrounded answer -- confidently discussing shipping timeframes as if they were relevant to a damage claim, when they aren't. The correct behavior, per the rag_qa_system_design lab: add an explicit similarity-score threshold check right after stage 1 -- if the top score (0.31) falls below a reasonable minimum threshold (e.g. 0.60, the value used in that lab's demo), skip stages 2 and 3's normal assembly-for-generation path entirely and instead surface an explicit "I don't have information covering that" response. This is a concrete illustration of why that threshold check belongs specifically at the retrieval stage of the pipeline, not somewhere later -- catching the problem here avoids ever handing the LLM a prompt built on a bad match in the first place, rather than hoping generation somehow "notices" the context doesn't really apply.
Problem 5 · Designing the pipeline's stage order and identifying the one hard dependency

Could stage 2 (budget check/compression) run BEFORE stage 1 (retrieval) instead of after? Explain the dependency using this lab's four stages, and name the one pair of stages that could, in principle, be reordered without breaking anything.

Show solution
No -- stage 2 cannot run before stage 1. Stage 2's entire job is checking whether the CANDIDATE CONTENT (the retrieved chunks plus history plus question) fits within budget, and deciding what to compress or truncate if it doesn't. Without stage 1 having already run, there IS no set of retrieved chunks yet to check the token cost of or potentially truncate -- stage 2 has nothing to operate on. This mirrors the rag_qa_system_design lab's point about the full pipeline being strictly sequential: each stage's output is a required input to the next, and stage 2 specifically requires stage 1's retrieved-chunks output as part of its own input. Similarly, stage 3 (assembly) requires stage 2's output (the budget-adjusted, possibly-compressed set of chunks and history) -- assembling BEFORE checking the budget would risk assembling something already known to be over budget, defeating the point of checking at all. And stage 4 (inspecting the result) obviously requires stage 3's assembled prompt to exist first. The one pair that COULD, in principle, be reordered without breaking anything: the conversation-history compression check and the retrieved-chunk relevance filtering both ultimately feed into the same stage-2 budget check, and depending on implementation, deciding "does history need compressing" doesn't strictly require having already decided "which chunks did retrieval return" (and vice versa) -- these two sub-decisions, both happening logically WITHIN stage 2, are themselves independent of each other, even though stage 2 as a whole still has to happen after stage 1 and before stage 3. This is a useful distinction for a system-design conversation: the four NAMED stages have a strict, unavoidable order, but that doesn't mean every sub-decision inside a given stage is itself ordered relative to every other sub-decision in that same stage.
