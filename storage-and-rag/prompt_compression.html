01 — Verbatim doesn't scale, but the gist mostly does

Summarizing older context

Start here, before any mechanics. Imagine keeping a diary of every conversation you've ever had with a close friend, written out word for word. After a few years, you'd have a shelf of notebooks — technically complete, but impractical to actually reread before your next conversation with them. What you'd realistically do instead is remember the GIST: "we talked about their job change, their trip to Portugal, and that ongoing disagreement with their sibling" — a few sentences that capture what mattered, discarding the exact wording of every single exchange. Most of the time, that's genuinely enough to pick the conversation back up naturally.

Prompt compression applies this same idea to a growing conversation history. As a conversation accumulates turns, keeping every past message verbatim in the context window eventually becomes unaffordable — it's exactly the token-budget pressure from the previous lab. Instead of keeping everything, a common strategy is: periodically, once history crosses some threshold (a turn count, or a token count), take the OLDER portion of the conversation and replace it with a single, shorter summary — typically generated by asking the LLM itself to condense those older turns into a few sentences that preserve the gist (key facts, decisions, open questions) — while keeping the most RECENT turns verbatim, since those are most likely to still be directly relevant to the current exchange.

# the compression shape: old turns -> one summary, recent turns stay verbatim def compress_if_needed(history, threshold_turns=10, keep_recent=3): if len(history) <= threshold_turns: return history # still short enough, no compression needed yet older = history[:-keep_recent] # everything except the most recent N turns recent = history[-keep_recent:] # kept verbatim summary = llm_summarize(older) # an extra LLM call, condensing older into a gist return [{"role": "system", "content": "Summary of earlier conversation: " + summary}] + recent
Interview-ready definition: prompt compression periodically replaces the older portion of a growing conversation history with a shorter LLM-generated summary that preserves the gist — key facts, decisions, and open threads — while keeping the most recent turns verbatim in full. This keeps total context size roughly bounded as a conversation continues indefinitely, rather than growing without limit, at the cost of an extra summarization LLM call each time compression runs and, critically, at the cost of losing whatever specific detail the summary didn't preserve.
02 — The gist survives. The specific fact might not

The trade-off: compression saves space, but a detail can vanish

Start here, before the example. Back to the diary analogy: your gist-level memory ("we talked about their trip to Portugal") is genuinely useful for picking the conversation back up naturally — but if your friend later says "remember, I told you the exact dates I'm free in October," and your memory only kept the gist, that specific detail is just gone. You didn't forget it through carelessness — you never kept it in a form your gist-level summary was capable of preserving in the first place. This is the real, structural trade-off of compression, not a bug to be engineered away: a summary is, by definition, LESS information than what it summarizes, and the compressor has to guess in advance what will and won't matter later.

A SPECIFIC DETAIL, BEFORE AND AFTER COMPRESSION

Before compression — raw message, turn 3

"My account ID is AX-7734-Q, and I need the refund processed to the card ending in 4471, not my original payment method."

After compression — the summary, turns 1-8

"User discussed a refund request, mentioned wanting it sent to a different card than originally used, and asked follow-up questions about processing time."

The summary correctly preserves the GIST — there's a refund, going to a different card. But the exact account ID (AX-7734-Q) and the exact last-four digits (4471) are gone. If the user later asks "can you confirm you're sending it to the right card," the model — now only seeing the summary, not the original message — has no way to recover those specific values. They weren't merely de-prioritized; they were never captured in the compressed form at all.

Why this isn't a fixable bug, just a real trade-off: a summarizer that tried to preserve every specific number, name, and exact phrase from the original messages wouldn't actually be compressing anything — it would just be a slightly reworded copy, defeating the entire point of freeing up token budget. Genuine compression requires the summarizer to make judgment calls about what's "gist" versus "specific detail," and it will sometimes guess wrong about which specific details turn out to matter later. This is precisely why systems that compress conversation history often also keep certain high-value facts (an account ID, a confirmed order number) pinned in a separate, never-compressed slot rather than trusting them to survive summarization at all.
Interview-ready definition: the core trade-off of prompt compression is that it exchanges recoverable, exact information for space — a summary preserves the general shape of what was discussed but, by construction, discards details the summarization step judged non-essential, and once the original verbatim turns are gone from context, those discarded details are genuinely unrecoverable from the summary alone. This isn't a solvable bug in the summarization prompt; it's an inherent property of lossy compression, which is why production systems often pair conversation compression with a separate mechanism (pinned facts, a structured memory store, or simply re-asking the user) for the small set of details that must never be allowed to depend on surviving a summary.

Problem 1 · Computing the space savings from compression

A conversation has 12 messages averaging 250 tokens each. After compression, the older 9 are replaced by a single 200-token summary, and the most recent 3 (250 tokens each) stay verbatim. What's the token count before and after, and what percentage reduction does this represent?

Show solution
Before: 12 messages x 250 tokens = 3,000 tokens. After: 1 summary (200 tokens) + 3 recent verbatim messages (3 x 250 = 750 tokens) = 950 tokens. Reduction: (3,000 - 950) / 3,000 ≈ 68% fewer tokens. This is the same shape as the before/after comparison in section 3 (3,000 tokens down to roughly 800-950 depending on exact summary length) -- the space savings come almost entirely from replacing the 9 OLDEST messages (2,250 tokens worth) with a single 200-token summary, a more than 11x compression ratio on just that older portion, while the recent 3 messages are left completely untouched since they stayed verbatim in both cases.
Problem 2 · Identifying what a summary would and wouldn't preserve

Given the same raw message from section 2 ("My account ID is AX-7734-Q, and I need the refund processed to the card ending in 4471, not my original payment method"), write what you'd expect a REASONABLE summarizer to keep versus drop, and explain why the drop is not a summarizer bug.

Show solution
A reasonable summarizer would likely KEEP: the general topic (a refund request) and the general instruction (send to a different card than originally used, not the original payment method) -- these are the kind of "gist" facts a summary is specifically designed to preserve, since they represent the shape of what was discussed and decided. It would likely DROP: the exact account ID string ("AX-7734-Q") and the exact last-four digits ("4471") -- these are precisely the kind of specific, low-level identifiers that a gist-level summary is not designed to retain, since summarization's whole purpose is condensing detail down to what's generally relevant, and specific IDs read as "detail" rather than "gist" to a summarization process with no way to know in advance that THIS specific number will be needed again later. This is not a summarizer bug because there's no way to summarize "compress this down significantly" while ALSO guaranteeing every specific number survives -- those two goals are in direct tension, and any summarizer that tried to satisfy both would end up not compressing much at all. The correct fix, per section 2's callout, isn't a better prompt for the summarizer -- it's not relying on compression to preserve values like account IDs in the first place, e.g. by pinning them in a separate, never-compressed slot.
Problem 3 · Choosing keep_recent and threshold_turns for a support-bot use case

A customer support chatbot's users often reference something they said "a couple messages ago" but almost never something from more than 5-6 turns back. Using section 1's compress_if_needed pseudocode, would you set keep_recent higher or lower than a general-purpose assistant, and why?

Show solution
keep_recent should be set HIGHER than a bare-minimum default (e.g. 5-6 rather than 2-3) specifically because this use case's actual reference pattern -- users pointing back "a couple messages ago," with essentially no references beyond 5-6 turns back -- defines exactly how far back verbatim (uncompressed) context needs to reach to reliably cover real user behavior. If keep_recent were set too low (e.g. 2), a user referencing something from 4 turns back would find that detail already folded into the lossy summary, right in the exact failure mode section 2 describes, even though the reference was well within this particular product's normal usage pattern. Setting keep_recent to roughly match (or slightly exceed) the observed "how far back do users actually reference things" distribution is the right way to size this parameter -- not a fixed number chosen in the abstract, but one informed by this specific product's real usage, the same way the token-budget lab's problem 3 emphasized recency/relevance patterns specific to the actual workload. threshold_turns (when compression triggers at all) is a separate, more independent knob -- it can stay whatever balances token savings against summarization call frequency/cost, since it doesn't interact with the "how far back do users reference things" concern the way keep_recent directly does.
Problem 4 · Diagnosing a bug where the pinned account ID still got lost

A team adds a "pinned facts" mechanism per section 2's callout — important details like account IDs get extracted and stored separately, immune to compression. Despite this, a user's account ID still vanished from context after a compression event. On investigation, the pinning step runs by asking the LLM to "extract important facts from the conversation so far" AFTER compression has already happened, not before. What's wrong, and what's the fix?

Show solution
The bug is a straightforward ordering problem: if the "extract important facts to pin" step runs AFTER compression, it's extracting facts from the ALREADY-COMPRESSED context -- meaning it's trying to pin details from the summary, not from the original raw messages. But the summary itself may have already dropped the account ID (exactly as section 2 describes happening to gist-level summaries), so there's nothing left in the compressed context for the pinning step to find and extract -- it can't pin a detail that's already gone by the time it runs. The fix is an ordering fix: pinning needs to happen BEFORE compression, scanning the RAW, still-verbatim messages for values worth protecting (account IDs, confirmed order numbers, specific dates/amounts) and storing them in the separate pinned-facts slot first -- only then should the older messages be handed to the summarizer and compressed. This way, the pinned facts mechanism is working from the same full-detail source the summarizer is compressing FROM, not from the summarizer's already-lossy output, which is exactly the distinction that makes "pin it separately" an actual fix rather than just another lossy step layered on top of the first one.
Problem 5 · Explaining why re-summarizing an already-compressed summary compounds the problem

A long-running conversation compresses once at turn 12 (turns 1-9 into a summary, turns 10-12 kept verbatim), then keeps going and compresses AGAIN at turn 24 — this time summarizing the PREVIOUS summary plus turns 10-21 together into one new summary. Why does this repeated-compression pattern make information loss worse over time than a single one-time compression would?

Show solution
Each compression pass is independently lossy -- the first pass already discarded whatever the summarizer judged as non-gist detail from turns 1-9. When the SECOND compression pass runs, it's summarizing the FIRST summary (which is already a lossy, reduced version of turns 1-9) together with turns 10-21 -- meaning the already-compressed content gets compressed AGAIN, on top of whatever it already lost the first time around. This compounds rather than merely repeats the loss: the second summarizer has no access to the original turns 1-9 raw text at all (it was already discarded after the first compression) -- it can only work from what the first summary preserved, and its own summarization pass can further compress or drop details that DID survive the first pass but don't make the cut for the second, more aggressive round of condensing. Each additional compression pass is compounding information loss on top of already-lossy input, rather than each pass independently starting from the full original detail -- which is exactly why very long-running conversations that compress repeatedly tend to retain less and less of the earliest parts of the conversation over time, even though each individual compression step, viewed in isolation, might look like a reasonable, modest amount of loss. This is a genuine argument for the "pin high-value facts before they can ever be compressed" strategy from section 2 and problem 4 -- pinned facts, stored outside the compression pipeline entirely, don't degrade further on repeated compression passes the way anything living only inside the rolling summary does.
