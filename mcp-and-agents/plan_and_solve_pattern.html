MCP & AGENTS LAB · PLAN-AND-SOLVE PATTERN

Commit to the whole route before the first step, instead of deciding one turn at a time.

Plan-and-Solve flips ReAct's core assumption: instead of interleaving reasoning and acting one step at a time, the model first writes out a complete multi-step plan, then executes each step in order. This lab solves the exact same worked example from react_pattern.html this way instead, then runs both approaches side by side on the same task so the structural difference is impossible to miss.

01 — Decide the whole route, then drive it

Planning upfront, then executing the plan

Start here, before any code. Think about the difference between driving somewhere by checking your GPS after every single turn versus looking at the full route once before you leave and then just driving it. The second approach — Plan-and-Solve — commits to a complete sequence of steps BEFORE taking the first action, then works through that sequence mechanically, step by step, generally without re-deciding what to do at each point the way react_pattern.html's loop does.

Applied to the exact same question from the ReAct lab — "What's the population of the capital of France?" — Plan-and-Solve produces a full plan FIRST, in one model call, before any tool has been invoked at all:

Plan: 1. Look up the capital of France. 2. Look up the population of whatever city step 1 returns. 3. Combine both facts into a final answer. -- plan is now FIXED. Execution begins, following it in order -- Step 1: lookup("capital of France") -> Paris Step 2: lookup("population of Paris") -> approximately 2.1 million Step 3: Combine -> "The capital of France is Paris, population approximately 2.1 million."

Notice something subtle in step 2 of the PLAN: it says "whatever city step 1 returns" rather than naming Paris directly — the plan is allowed to reference a PLACEHOLDER for a future result, because the plan is written before that result exists. This is different from ReAct, where Thought 2 is generated only AFTER Observation 1 is already known, so it can just say "Paris" directly. Plan-and-Solve's plan has to be written in a way that tolerates not yet knowing every concrete value it will operate on.

Interview-ready definition: Plan-and-Solve is an agent pattern where the model first generates a complete, ordered, multi-step plan for solving a task in a single reasoning pass — before any tool has been called — and then a (typically simpler, more mechanical) execution phase carries out each step of that plan in sequence, substituting in real results as they become available. This separates the "what should happen, in what order" decision (made once, upfront) from the "actually do it" phase (mostly mechanical step execution), in contrast to ReAct's single loop that re-decides the next action after every observation.
02 — Coherent and reviewable, but can't adapt mid-flight

Trade-offs against ReAct's interleaved approach

Start here, before the table. A written travel itinerary you can hand to someone else and have them check before you leave is genuinely useful — they can spot "wait, step 4 assumes the airport is still open at that hour" before you're already at the airport. But if a flight gets cancelled mid-trip, that same fixed itinerary doesn't help you improvise — you have to go back and re-plan from scratch, or abandon the plan and start reacting turn by turn instead. Plan-and-Solve and ReAct trade almost exactly this pair of strengths for each other.

Plan-and-Solve

Strength: the full strategy exists as one coherent artifact before execution starts — genuinely easier to review, approve, or reject as a whole ("does this plan even make sense?") than judging an in-progress ReAct loop one step at a time.

Weakness: if an early step's actual result changes what the RIGHT next step should be, the fixed plan has no built-in mechanism to notice or adapt — it just keeps executing the steps as originally written, even if they've become wrong.

ReAct

Strength: every action is chosen in light of the most recent observation, so it naturally adapts step by step when something unexpected happens — no separate re-planning phase needed, the very next Thought just accounts for it.

Weakness: because there's no upfront overall strategy, a long task can lose the overall thread — the model is only ever reasoning about "what's the next single step," with no persistent artifact expressing the big-picture plan for a human (or the model itself) to check against.

Where Plan-and-Solve's weakness bites concretely: imagine the plan's step 1 was "look up the capital of France" but the tool instead returned an error, or returned "Paris (former capital; current administrative capital disputed)" — an unexpected shape of result the plan's step 2 ("look up the population of whatever step 1 returns") didn't anticipate. A rigid Plan-and-Solve executor would either crash trying to feed a malformed value into step 2, or blindly proceed with a wrong assumption — neither is "the model reasoning fresh about what changed," the way ReAct's next Thought naturally would. Production Plan-and-Solve systems address this with an explicit REPLAN step triggered when execution diverges from what the plan expected — but that's an added mechanism, not something the base pattern provides for free.
Interview-ready definition: Plan-and-Solve trades ReAct's step-by-step adaptivity for upfront coherence and reviewability — a fixed plan is easier to inspect, approve, or reject as a whole before any side effects occur, and can reduce redundant re-reasoning on long tasks with a clear overall strategy. Its corresponding weakness is that the plan doesn't automatically adjust when an early step's real-world result diverges from what was assumed when the plan was written, requiring an explicit re-planning mechanism to recover — whereas ReAct adapts for free at the cost of having no persistent, reviewable strategy and being more prone to losing the overall thread on longer, many-step tasks.

Problem 1 · Writing a plan that tolerates an unknown future value

Section 1's plan step 2 says "look up the population of whatever city step 1 returns" rather than naming a city directly. Why is this phrasing necessary for Plan-and-Solve specifically, when it wouldn't be necessary for ReAct's equivalent step?

Show solution
Plan-and-Solve's plan is written in ONE pass, entirely before execution begins -- at the moment step 2 is being written, step 1 hasn't actually run yet, so the specific city name (Paris) genuinely isn't known yet. The plan has no choice but to refer to it indirectly ("whatever step 1 returns") because the concrete value doesn't exist at plan-writing time -- this is a structural consequence of committing to the full sequence upfront, per section 1. ReAct's Thought 2, by contrast, is generated AFTER Observation 1 has already arrived (react_pattern.html, section 1) -- by the time Thought 2 is written, the model already knows the capital is Paris and can just say so directly, with no placeholder needed. This difference is a direct, necessary consequence of WHEN each pattern generates its reasoning about step 2 relative to when step 1's actual result becomes available -- it's not a stylistic choice, it's forced by each pattern's structure.
Problem 2 · Diagnosing what breaks when a plan's assumption turns out wrong

A Plan-and-Solve plan's step 2 is "cross-reference the population figure against the 2020 census database." Execution reaches step 2 and discovers no 2020 census database is available as a tool — only a "general web lookup" tool exists. What are the executor's actual options, and why does this scenario not arise the same way in ReAct?

Show solution
The executor's options, given section 2's trade-off table, are limited and all somewhat unsatisfying compared to ReAct's natural handling: (a) fail the step outright and abort the whole plan, (b) skip step 2 and proceed to step 3 anyway, potentially producing an incomplete or wrong final answer, or (c) trigger an explicit REPLAN -- going back to the model with "step 2 as written can't execute; here's what tools actually exist; produce a revised plan" -- which section 2's warning callout notes is an ADDED mechanism, not something base Plan-and-Solve provides automatically. None of these is "just reason about it and adapt," because the executor carrying out a fixed plan's steps is often a comparatively mechanical component, not necessarily a full reasoning pass at every step. This scenario doesn't arise the same way in ReAct because ReAct never commits to a specific tool or approach for "step 2" before knowing what tools are actually available and what step 1 actually returned -- the model choosing Action 2 already has full knowledge of the available toolset and Observation 1's actual content at the moment it decides what to do next, so it would simply never have proposed calling a nonexistent "2020 census database" tool in the first place. Plan-and-Solve's vulnerability here is specifically that its plan was committed to before that same knowledge was necessarily verified against reality.
Problem 3 · Choosing the right pattern for a reviewable, high-stakes task

A task involves an agent that will delete old records from a production database as part of a cleanup job, and a human wants to approve the exact sequence of operations BEFORE anything executes. Which pattern fits better, and why does the other pattern make this specific requirement hard to satisfy?

Show solution
Plan-and-Solve fits distinctly better here, for exactly the reason section 2's comparison table highlights: it produces a full plan as a concrete artifact BEFORE any action (in this case, any deletion) actually happens, which is precisely what a human-approval-gate workflow needs to hook into -- "show me the plan, I approve or reject it, THEN execution begins" maps directly onto Plan-and-Solve's two-phase structure. ReAct makes this requirement hard to satisfy because there is no single point in its execution where "the whole strategy" exists as an artifact to review -- by the time a human could see what the FIRST action is going to be, that action (a real deletion, in this scenario) may already be about to execute, and the SECOND action doesn't even exist yet as a concept until after the first one's observation comes back. Approving "the whole plan" in ReAct would require either running it entirely in a dry-run/simulated mode first and treating that as the reviewable artifact, or approving individual actions one at a time as they're proposed -- both change ReAct's fundamental one-step-at-a-time execution model rather than simply exposing something it already produces naturally, unlike Plan-and-Solve's plan.
Problem 4 · Estimating relative cost for a long, mostly-independent task

A task requires looking up 10 unrelated facts (none depends on any other's result) and combining them into a report. Using section 2's cost row, roughly compare the number of full model reasoning calls each pattern would need, and explain the gap.

Show solution
Plan-and-Solve: roughly 1 model call to produce the full 10-step plan, plus execution of the 10 lookups -- and because none of the 10 facts depends on any other, the EXECUTION steps here can often be simple, mechanical tool calls (or even dispatched in parallel) rather than requiring 10 separate full reasoning passes, since the plan already specified exactly what to look up for each. Very roughly: 1 reasoning call + 10 mechanical executions. ReAct: because every single step requires a fresh Thought generated by a full model call before the next Action, and section 2's table notes ReAct "re-reasons from scratch" at every step, this task costs roughly 10 separate Thought-generating model calls (one per lookup), each re-sending a growing transcript per the react_pattern lab's Problem 3 -- meaningfully more total model reasoning than Plan-and-Solve needed here, because ReAct's adaptivity is being paid for on every single step even though (as react_pattern's own Problem 4 notes) none of these particular lookups actually needed it, since they're mutually independent. This is exactly the scenario where Plan-and-Solve's upfront-planning cost structure wins economically -- the savings come specifically from NOT needing a fresh full reasoning pass for each of the 10 independent steps.
Problem 5 · Designing a hybrid that keeps both strengths

Sketch, at a high level, how you'd combine Plan-and-Solve's upfront reviewability with ReAct's ability to adapt mid-task — without simply picking one pattern and accepting its weakness.

Show solution
A reasonable hybrid: generate an initial plan upfront (Plan-and-Solve's reviewable artifact, satisfying Problem 3's approval-gate requirement), but execute each individual STEP of that plan using a bounded ReAct-style loop rather than a purely mechanical executor -- so each step can still adapt to its own step-level surprises (a tool erroring, an unexpected result shape) using the same Thought/Action/Observation reasoning react_pattern.html covers, without needing to re-derive the entire higher-level strategy from scratch. Critically, this hybrid ALSO needs an explicit REPLAN trigger condition: if a step's actual result diverges enough from what the ORIGINAL plan assumed (not just a step-level hiccup a mini-ReAct loop can absorb, but something that invalidates a LATER step's premise entirely -- e.g. Problem 2's missing-tool scenario), the system detects this and goes back to the model with "here's the original plan, here's what actually happened, produce a revised plan for the remaining steps" rather than blindly continuing to execute steps written against assumptions that are now known to be false. This gets Plan-and-Solve's upfront reviewability and Plan-and-Solve's efficiency on steps that don't need per-step adaptivity, while still getting ReAct's local resilience within a step and an explicit (not automatic, but available) path to global re-planning when reality diverges enough to matter -- the cost is real added complexity (deciding what counts as "diverged enough to replan" is itself a nontrivial design decision), which is exactly why plenty of real systems still choose one pure pattern or the other rather than this hybrid.
