DATA STRUCTURES & ALGORITHMS LAB · TREE ROTATIONS & BALANCING

A rotation doesn't move data — it re-parents three pointers and the whole subtree follows.

The previous lab ended on a cliffhanger: an unbalanced BST degrades to O(n). Rotations are the fix — a local, O(1) restructuring move that changes which node is "on top" of a small piece of the tree without breaking the BST property anywhere. This lab animates an actual rotation frame by frame, node positions moving in real time, then walks all four AVL imbalance cases and the Red-Black invariant that gives the same O(log n) guarantee a different way.

01 — Watch three pointers change, live

Right rotation, step by step

Start here, before any code. Imagine a company org chart where a manager (call them P for "parent") has one direct report (L for "left child") who is completely overloaded because half the team reports through them instead of directly to P. A "right rotation" is a reorg: L becomes the new manager, P becomes L's direct report instead, and the sub-team L used to manage on their right side (people who were "too big" for P to hold alongside L) get reassigned to P, who now has room for them. Nobody's data changed — only who reports to whom.

Concretely, for a right rotation around a node P with left child L: L becomes the new subtree root, P becomes L's right child, and L's ORIGINAL right subtree (call it T2) — which holds values between L and P — gets reattached as P's new left child, since those values are still correctly positioned as greater than L and less than P.

RIGHT ROTATION — FULL ANIMATED STEP-THROUGH
def rotate_right(P): L = P.left # L will become the new subtree root T2 = L.right # L's right subtree — values between L and P L.right = P # P becomes L's right child P.left = T2 # T2 reattaches as P's left child (still valid: L < T2 < P) return L # L is the new root of this subtree — caller must # re-link L into P's OLD parent
Interview-ready definition: a right rotation around node P promotes P's left child L to P's former position, demotes P to L's right child, and reparents L's original right subtree as P's new left child — a constant-time (O(1)) pointer rewiring of exactly three references that preserves the BST property while decreasing the height of the left-heavy side by one and increasing the height of the (now L's) right side by one.
02 — The mirror image

Left rotation

A left rotation is the exact mirror: applied around a node P with right child R, R becomes the new subtree root, P becomes R's left child, and R's original left subtree reattaches as P's new right child.

LEFT ROTATION — FULL ANIMATED STEP-THROUGH
def rotate_left(P): R = P.right # R will become the new subtree root T2 = R.left # R's left subtree — values between P and R R.left = P # P becomes R's left child P.right = T2 # T2 reattaches as P's right child (still valid: P < T2 < R) return R # R is the new root of this subtree
03 — The number that triggers a rotation

Balance factor and when AVL trees rotate

Start here, before any code. Think of the balance factor as a carpenter's level for each node: it tells you whether that node's "load" is leaning left, leaning right, or sitting flat. An AVL tree checks this level after every insert or delete, and the moment any node tips too far, it performs a rotation right there to level it back out — before the imbalance can compound further up the tree.

Formally: balance factor(node) = height(node.left) − height(node.right). An empty subtree has height −1 (or some conventions use 0 for a leaf's children; the important part is the relative comparison). AVL trees maintain the invariant that every node's balance factor is in {-1, 0, 1} — if an insert or delete pushes any node's balance factor to -2 or +2, a rotation (or pair of rotations) is triggered at that node to restore the invariant.

Why check after every single insert/delete, not periodically: imbalance compounds. A node at +2 today can push an ancestor to +3 tomorrow if left unaddressed, and by then a single rotation may no longer be enough to fix it (multiple rotations, or a completely different rebalancing point, might be needed). Checking and correcting immediately after each modification means at most one node is ever off-balance at a time, and it's always fixable with 1-2 rotations.
04 — Diagnosing which fix applies

LL, RR, LR, RL rotation cases

When a node's balance factor hits ±2, which rotation(s) fix it depends on the SHAPE of the imbalance, not just its direction. The naming describes the path from the unbalanced node down to where the extra height actually lives: "LL" means the imbalance is in the left child's left subtree, "LR" means it's in the left child's right subtree, and so on.

LL case — left-left, single RIGHT rotation

→

Extra height is in the left child's LEFT subtree. One right rotation around the unbalanced node fixes it directly.

RR case — right-right, single LEFT rotation

→

Mirror of LL. Extra height is in the right child's RIGHT subtree. One left rotation fixes it.

LR case — left-right, LEFT then RIGHT rotation

→

Extra height is in the left child's RIGHT subtree — a single right rotation alone would NOT fix this (the zigzag shape defeats it). First left-rotate the left child to convert this into an LL shape, then right-rotate the top node.

RL case — right-left, RIGHT then LEFT rotation

→

Mirror of LR. First right-rotate the right child to convert this into an RR shape, then left-rotate the top node.

The single most common mistake: applying only one rotation to an LR or RL case. Because the extra weight zigzags (left-then-right, or right-then-left) instead of running straight down one side, a single rotation at the top node just relocates the imbalance instead of fixing it — the inner rotation is required first to straighten the zigzag into a straight line, THEN the outer rotation resolves it.
05 — A looser invariant, same guarantee

Red-Black trees — the invariant, conceptually

Start here, before any code. AVL trees are like a strict fire code that requires every room's exits to be within one step of perfectly even — very safe, but you're re-checking and adjusting constantly. A Red-Black tree is a looser building code: it doesn't require every path to be nearly equal, just that no path is ever more than twice as long as any other path from the same point. That looser bar means fewer rotations are needed on average, at the cost of the tree being allowed to be somewhat less tightly balanced than an AVL tree.

Every node is colored red or black, and the tree maintains these rules:

Red node Black node
RuleWhat it prevents
Root is always blackSimplifies edge-case handling at the top
Red node's children are always black (no two reds in a row)Prevents a path from being dominated by red nodes and getting arbitrarily "loose"
Every path from a node to its descendant None leaves passes through the same number of BLACK nodes ("black-height")This is the core invariant — it caps how much longer one path can be than another

The "no red-red in a row" rule plus "equal black-height on every path" together guarantee that the longest possible path (alternating red-black-red-black...) is at most twice the length of the shortest possible path (all black). That factor-of-2 cap is enough to guarantee height stays O(log n), even though individual paths can differ in length — unlike AVL's much tighter requirement that heights differ by at most 1 at every node.

06 — Tying it back to Big-O

Why balancing guarantees O(log n) operations

Recall from the previous lab: BST operations cost O(h), where h is the tree's height — and an unconstrained BST can degrade to h = n. Both AVL and Red-Black trees add a rule that mathematically bounds h in terms of n:

  • AVL: the balance-factor invariant guarantees h ≤ ~1.44 × log₂(n) — provably logarithmic, with a small constant factor.
  • Red-Black: the black-height invariant guarantees h ≤ 2 × log₂(n+1) — also logarithmic, with a slightly larger (but still constant) factor.

Because a rotation is O(1) (three pointer writes) and at most O(log n) rotations are ever needed to fully rebalance after a single insert or delete (rebalancing only needs to walk back up the path you just inserted/deleted along, which has length h = O(log n)), the total cost of a self-balancing insert or delete is O(log n) for the descent, plus O(log n) for the rebalancing check, plus O(1) per rotation actually performed — all of which is O(log n) overall.

Interview-ready definition: self-balancing trees (AVL, Red-Black) guarantee O(log n) search, insert, and delete by maintaining an invariant — checked and restored after every modification via O(1) rotations — that bounds the tree's height to O(log n) regardless of insertion order, in contrast to a plain BST where height (and therefore per-operation cost) is a function of insertion order and can degrade to O(n) in the worst case.

Problem 1 · Trace a right rotation by hand

Node P=30 has left child L=20. L has a right child T2=25 and a left child 10. Perform rotate_right(P) by hand: what is the new subtree root, and what are the final left/right children of each of P, L, and T2?

Show solution
New subtree root: L (20) Before: 30(P) / 20(L) / \ 10 25(T2) rotate_right(P=30): L = P.left = 20 T2 = L.right = 25 L.right = P → 20's right child becomes 30 P.left = T2 → 30's left child becomes 25 (T2) return L → 20 is the new subtree root After: 20(L) / \ 10 30(P) / 25(T2) Final state: L(20)'s left child is still 10 (untouched), L(20)'s right child is P(30). P(30)'s left child is T2(25), P(30)'s right child is whatever it was before (unchanged, not involved in this rotation).
Problem 2 · Compute balance factors

A node X has a left subtree of height 3 and a right subtree of height 1. Compute X's balance factor. Is X AVL-valid? If not, is this an LL-family or RR-family imbalance, and why?

Show solution
balance_factor(X) = height(left) - height(right) = 3 - 1 = +2 +2 is outside the valid AVL range of {-1, 0, 1} — X is NOT AVL-valid, it needs rebalancing. +2 (positive, left-heavy) means the imbalance is on the LEFT side — this is in the "LL-family" (could be pure LL or LR depending on whether the extra height within that left subtree leans further left or right — you'd need to check the left child's OWN balance factor to determine which of LL or LR applies, and therefore whether one rotation or two is needed).
Problem 3 · Identify the case and the fix

Inserting 30, then 20, then 10 (in that order) into an empty BST with no rebalancing would produce a straight line leaning left. At the moment 10 is inserted, node 30 becomes unbalanced. Is this LL, RR, LR, or RL? What single fix resolves it?

Show solution
LL case. The tree right before rebalancing: 30 / 20 / 10 The extra height is in 30's left child (20)'s LEFT subtree (10) — a straight line down the left-left path. This is the textbook LL shape. Fix: a single right rotation around 30. Result: 20 / \ 10 30 Now balanced (each node's balance factor is 0).
Problem 4 · Why LR needs two rotations

Node P=30 is left-heavy with left child L=10, and L's right child is 20 (a "zigzag" — LR shape). Explain concretely why a single right rotation around P does NOT fix the imbalance, then describe the two-rotation fix.

Show solution
Before: 30(P) / 10(L) \ 20 A single right rotation around P promotes L (10) to the top: 10 \ 30 / 20 This is now RIGHT-heavy at 10 (its right subtree has height 2, left has height 0) — the imbalance didn't get fixed, it just flipped which side is heavy and moved to a different node. Still broken. Correct two-step fix: Step 1 — left-rotate L (10) first: this promotes 20 to be P's new left child, with 10 becoming 20's left child. Now the shape is: 30(P) / 20 / 10 This is now a clean LL shape. Step 2 — right-rotate P (30) as in the LL case: 20 becomes the new subtree root, with 10 as its left child and 30 as its right child: 20 / \ 10 30 Balanced. The inner rotation was necessary specifically to straighten the zigzag into a single direction before the outer rotation could resolve it.
Problem 5 · Red-Black black-height reasoning

A Red-Black tree path has 4 black nodes and 0 red nodes (length 4). Another path in the same tree has 4 black nodes and 3 red nodes alternating with them (length 7). Are both paths valid under the Red-Black invariants? What is the maximum possible length of any path in this tree, given a shortest path of 4?

Show solution
Yes, both are valid — the invariant requires equal BLACK-height (both have exactly 4 black nodes), not equal total length. Red nodes are allowed to appear as long as no two reds are adjacent, so length can vary based on how many reds are woven in. Maximum possible length: since no two red nodes can be adjacent, red nodes can appear at most every other position, meaning a path can have at most as many red nodes as black nodes. With black-height 4, the longest possible path alternates black-red-black-red-black-red-black-red = 4 black + 4 red = 8... but more precisely the bound given is "at most 2x the shortest path": shortest = 4 (all black), so longest ≤ 2 × 4 = 8. This factor-of-2 cap (not exact equality) is what still guarantees O(log n) height even without AVL's tighter per-node balance requirement.
Problem 6 · Full worked example: insert causing an RL case

Starting with a balanced AVL subtree rooted at 10 with right child 30, insert 20. Show the resulting shape before rebalancing, identify the case, and perform the fix.

Show solution
Before insert: 10 \ 30 Insert 20: 20 < 30, so it becomes 30's left child (BST insert rule): 10 \ 30 / 20 Check balance factor of 10: height(left)=−1(empty), height(right)=1(the 30-20 subtree) → balance factor = −1 − 1 = −2. Unbalanced, right-heavy. Shape of the imbalance: right child (30)'s LEFT subtree (20) has the extra height — this is the RL case (right-left zigzag). Fix, step 1 — right-rotate 30 (the right child) first: promotes 20 above 30 on that side: 10 \ 20 \ 30 Now a clean RR shape. Fix, step 2 — left-rotate 10 (the original unbalanced node): 20 / \ 10 30 Balanced — every node's balance factor is 0.
