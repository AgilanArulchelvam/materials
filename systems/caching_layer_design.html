Every caching decision is a bet on how wrong a stale answer is allowed to be.

Caching isn't one design choice — it's several independent ones stacked together: when does a write actually reach the backing store (write-through vs write-back), what gets evicted when the cache fills up (LRU vs LFU), and how stale is the cached copy allowed to get before that staleness becomes a real problem (consistency trade-offs). This lab animates both write strategies side by side, evicts a real LRU cache live, and connects the whole thing back to the cache-invalidation difficulty from the CDNs lab.

01 — When does a write actually become durable?

Write-through vs write-back caching

Start here, before any diagrams. Imagine a hotel front desk that also keeps a small personal notepad of guest requests. One approach: every time a guest makes a request, the desk clerk writes it on the notepad AND immediately walks it over to the hotel's permanent guest-services ledger before telling the guest "done" — slower for the guest, because two things had to happen before they got their confirmation, but the request is guaranteed to survive even if the notepad is later lost. The other approach: the clerk writes it on the notepad, tells the guest "done" immediately, and only copies notepad entries into the permanent ledger later, in a batch, whenever convenient — much faster for the guest, but if the notepad gets lost before that batch copy happens, those requests are gone with no trace in the permanent ledger.

Write-through caching is the first approach: every write goes to the cache AND synchronously to the backing store (the database, the source of truth) before the write is acknowledged as complete. The caller waits for BOTH writes to finish. This guarantees the cache and the backing store are never out of sync — if the cache is lost or evicted a moment later, the backing store already has the exact same data, so nothing is lost. The cost is latency: every write pays for two writes' worth of time, not one, since the slower of the two (usually the backing store) is on the critical path.

Write-back (also called write-behind) caching is the second approach: a write goes to the cache only, and is acknowledged as complete immediately — the backing store is updated LATER, asynchronously, either on a timer, in a batch, or when that cache entry is eventually evicted. This is much faster for the caller, since only the (typically much faster) cache write is on the critical path. The real risk: if the cache fails, crashes, or loses that entry before the deferred write to the backing store ever happens, that write is lost permanently — the backing store never learns about it, because nothing durable ever recorded it outside the cache.

Write-through — safer, slower

Cache and backing store updated synchronously, together, before acknowledging. No window where the two can diverge. Every write pays the backing store's latency directly.

Write-back — faster, riskier

Only the cache is updated before acknowledging; the backing store catches up later. Writes are fast, but there's a real window where a cache failure loses data the backing store never received.

Interview-ready definition: write-through caching writes to both the cache and the backing store synchronously, acknowledging the write only after both succeed, guaranteeing the two stay consistent at the cost of write latency equal to the slower of the two writes. Write-back caching writes only to the cache, acknowledges immediately, and defers the backing-store write to later (batched, timed, or on eviction), trading a real risk of data loss on cache failure for write latency close to the cache's own speed alone. The choice is a direct trade between write latency and durability guarantees, and neither is universally correct — it depends on whether the data being written can tolerate being lost if the cache fails before flushing.
02 — What gets thrown out when the cache is full

Cache eviction policies: LRU and LFU

Start here, before the mechanics. Imagine a small bookshelf that only fits 5 books, but you keep wanting to bring home new ones. Two different rules for deciding which book to give away when the shelf is full: rule one, give away whichever book you haven't touched in the LONGEST time, regardless of how many times you've read it historically — recency is all that matters. Rule two, give away whichever book you've read the FEWEST total times ever, regardless of when you last touched it — total popularity is all that matters. Both are reasonable "make room" rules, but they can pick different books to give away for the exact same shelf.

LRU (Least Recently Used) evicts whichever cache entry hasn't been accessed for the longest time, tracking only recency of access — a typical implementation keeps entries ordered by last-access time (often via a doubly-linked list plus a hash map for O(1) access-and-reorder), moving an entry to the "most recent" end every time it's read or written, and evicting from the "least recent" end when the cache is full.

LFU (Least Frequently Used) evicts whichever cache entry has been accessed the fewest total times, tracking a hit counter per entry rather than recency — an entry accessed 500 times last week but not at all today can still outrank an entry accessed twice, once yesterday and once just now, under LFU, even though LRU would have evicted the 500-times entry first for being less RECENT.

03 — Perfect consistency and good performance can't both win

Cache consistency trade-offs

Start here, before the tension. A cache exists for exactly one reason: to avoid repeatedly paying the cost of asking the source of truth the same question. But the instant that source of truth changes, every cached copy of the old answer is now wrong, and there's a real question of HOW FAST that wrongness gets corrected — instantly, in a few seconds, or not until some longer window elapses. Making the cache correct instantly, every time, essentially means re-verifying with the source of truth on every single read — which defeats the entire performance purpose of having a cache in the first place.

This is the core tension of cache consistency: keeping a cache perfectly synchronized with its backing store costs performance directly, so real systems deliberately accept some amount of staleness in exchange for the speed a cache exists to provide. The question isn't "how do we achieve perfect consistency" (that's essentially "how do we make the cache pointless"), it's "how much staleness can this specific data tolerate, and what mechanism keeps that staleness bounded rather than unbounded."

This connects directly to cache invalidation being hard: the CDNs lab covered why invalidation is a genuinely hard DISTRIBUTED problem, not just a bookkeeping inconvenience — propagating "this changed" to every cache that might be holding a stale copy cannot be made instantaneous or perfectly reliable at scale. The same reasoning applies to any caching layer, not just CDN edge caches: TTL-based expiration is the same self-healing safety net here too (staleness is bounded to a known window even if active invalidation silently fails somewhere), and active invalidation still requires reliably reaching every cache instance holding the stale entry, which is exactly as fragile in a general caching layer as it is across CDN edge locations.

Concretely, the consistency spectrum a caching layer sits on looks like this: at one extreme, no caching at all (always read the source of truth — perfectly consistent, zero staleness, but zero performance benefit either). At the other extreme, cache forever with no invalidation and no TTL (maximum performance, but staleness is UNBOUNDED — a change to the source of truth might never be reflected in the cache at all). Real systems live somewhere in between: a TTL bounds staleness to "at most N seconds old," write-through keeps cache and store synchronized on the WRITE path specifically (trading write latency, as covered in section 1) while reads can still be served from a cache that might be stale relative to writes from OTHER paths, and active invalidation trims that window further for changes that can't wait out a TTL.

Interview-ready definition: cache consistency is the trade-off between how quickly a cache reflects changes made to its backing store and how much performance benefit the cache still provides — perfect, instantaneous consistency requires re-verifying with the source of truth on every access, which eliminates the cache's performance benefit entirely, so real systems deliberately accept a bounded staleness window (via TTLs, write-through on the write path, or active invalidation for urgent changes) rather than pursuing unattainable perfect real-time consistency. This is the same underlying tension the CDNs lab covers as cache invalidation being a hard distributed problem — a general caching layer faces the identical difficulty of reliably propagating "this is now stale" to every cached copy, not just at the CDN edge-server scale.

Problem 1 · Choosing write-through vs write-back for two systems

System A is a financial ledger recording account balance changes. System B is a view-count counter on a social media post, updated on every view, where losing a few recent increments on a rare cache failure is a non-issue. Which write strategy fits each, and why?

Show solution
System A (financial ledger): write-through. A lost write here means a financial transaction that the backing store never recorded -- an unacceptable outcome for a ledger, where every write must be durable the moment it's acknowledged. The added write latency (waiting for the backing store's write to complete, not just the cache's) is an acceptable cost given what's at stake if a write is silently lost. System B (view counter): write-back. Losing a handful of recent view increments on a rare cache failure is explicitly stated as a non-issue -- the counter doesn't need to be exactly precise, and the performance benefit of not waiting on the backing store for every single view event (which could be extremely high-frequency, e.g. thousands per second for a viral post) is worth far more than the small, tolerable risk of losing a few increments. This is exactly the kind of data where accepting write-back's durability risk in exchange for its speed is the correct engineering trade, not a shortcut.
Problem 2 · LRU vs LFU on a concrete access sequence

A 3-slot cache currently holds A (accessed 50 times total, last accessed 10 minutes ago), B (accessed 2 times total, last accessed 9 minutes ago), and C (accessed 1 time total, last accessed 8 minutes ago). A new item D arrives right now. Which item gets evicted under LRU? Under LFU? Explain the difference in reasoning.

Show solution
Under LRU: A gets evicted. LRU only tracks recency of last access, and A was last accessed 10 minutes ago -- longer ago than B (9 minutes) or C (8 minutes) -- regardless of the fact that A has been accessed by far the most total times (50, versus B's 2 and C's 1). LRU is completely blind to historical frequency; it only cares about "what was touched least recently." Under LFU: C gets evicted. LFU only tracks total access count, and C has been accessed just once -- fewer times than A (50) or B (2) -- regardless of the fact that C was the MOST recently accessed of the three (8 minutes ago, more recent than A's 10 or B's 9). LFU is completely blind to recency; it only cares about "what has been touched least often, ever." The difference in reasoning: this scenario is specifically constructed so that "least recently used" and "least frequently used" point at DIFFERENT items (A under LRU, C under LFU), which is exactly the kind of case where the two policies genuinely disagree -- a consistently popular item (A) that simply hasn't been touched in the last few minutes is exactly what LRU evicts and LFU protects, which is the cache-pollution scenario section 2's comparison table describes.
Problem 3 · Diagnosing a consistency bug from a stated symptom

A user updates their profile bio, sees the change reflected immediately in their own app, but a friend viewing their profile from a different app instance sees the OLD bio for another 45 seconds. The team says this is "expected behavior, not a bug." Using section 3's framing, explain why this could genuinely be an intentional design decision rather than an oversight.

Show solution
This is consistent with a deliberately bounded-staleness caching design, not necessarily a bug. If profile reads are served from a cache with, say, a 45-60 second TTL (rather than hitting the backing store on every profile view, which would be far more expensive at scale for a frequently-viewed resource like a user profile), then any cached copy already sitting in some other app instance's cache before the bio update happened will keep serving that stale bio until its TTL naturally expires -- exactly the "at most N seconds old" bounded staleness window described in section 3, not an unbounded or unpredictable failure. The user's OWN view updating immediately is also consistent with this: their own client likely bypassed the cache (or invalidated/refreshed their own locally cached copy) upon making the edit, which is a common pattern -- the WRITER sees their own write immediately (sometimes called read-your-writes consistency), while OTHER readers are still bound by the general cache TTL until it naturally expires or an active invalidation reaches their cache. Whether 45 seconds of staleness for non-authoring viewers is an ACCEPTABLE trade is a product decision (the team judged that a slightly stale bio for other viewers is a fine cost for the performance benefit of not re-fetching profile data on every view) -- which is exactly the "how much staleness can this data tolerate" question section 3 frames as the real design question, not "how do we make it perfectly consistent."
Problem 4 · Combining write-back with LFU and identifying the compounded risk

A team uses write-back caching combined with LFU eviction for a cache holding shopping cart contents. Explain a failure scenario where the interaction of these two specific choices (not either one alone) causes a worse outcome than either choice would cause independently.

Show solution
A concrete compounded scenario: a shopping cart item is added (a write, buffered only in the cache under write-back, not yet flushed to the backing store) for a brand-new, first-time visitor -- so this cart entry starts with a LOW access count (it's new; LFU has no accumulated frequency history for it yet). If the cache is under memory pressure and needs to evict something to make room for other entries, LFU is likely to select this brand-new, low-frequency cart entry for eviction BEFORE its write-back flush to the backing store has happened -- since LFU specifically targets low-frequency entries for eviction, and a just- created entry is definitionally low-frequency regardless of how important its unflushed data is. The compounded failure: under write-through, this specific risk wouldn't exist, because the cart addition would already be durably in the backing store the moment it was acknowledged -- eviction from the cache afterward would be harmless, since the backing store already has the data. Under write-back, eviction of an entry BEFORE its deferred flush happens is exactly the same "lost write" failure mode from section 1 -- but LFU specifically makes this MORE likely to happen to a fresh, important write than LRU would, because LRU would only evict this entry once it's no longer RECENT (giving the deferred flush more of a grace-period window in practice), while LFU can evict it almost immediately purely because it's new and hasn't accumulated hits yet, no matter how recently it was written. The fix here would be either switching to write-through for this specific cache (durability matters more than raw latency for cart contents), or ensuring evictions can never remove an entry that still has a pending, unflushed write-back.
Problem 5 · Choosing a caching strategy for a stated product requirement

A product requires: (1) writes must never be silently lost, (2) the workload has a strong recency bias (recently-viewed products are far more likely to be viewed again soon than old ones), and (3) a few seconds of read staleness for OTHER users viewing the same data is acceptable. Specify a full caching design: write strategy, eviction policy, and consistency mechanism, justifying each against the three requirements.

Show solution
Write strategy: write-through. Requirement (1) — writes must never be silently lost — directly rules out write-back's core risk (a cache failure losing an unflushed write). Write-through's added write latency is the acceptable cost for satisfying a hard durability requirement. Eviction policy: LRU. Requirement (2) — strong recency bias in the access pattern — is precisely the condition under which LRU outperforms LFU (section 2's comparison table): LRU directly optimizes for "recently touched items are likely to be touched again soon," which matches this workload's stated shape, and LRU's lower implementation overhead is a reasonable additional point in its favor when there's no strong reason (like a hot-set-with-recent-noise pattern) to pay LFU's extra bookkeeping cost. Consistency mechanism: a short TTL (a few seconds, matching requirement 3's explicit tolerance) as the primary bounded-staleness mechanism for READS by other users, since write-through already keeps the cache and backing store synchronized on the WRITE path itself — the TTL is really protecting against staleness from OTHER write paths or replicas, not covering for write-back's lack of synchronization (there is none here, since write-through was chosen). No need for aggressive active invalidation given requirement (3) explicitly tolerates a few seconds of staleness — that tolerance is exactly what a short TTL, as the cheaper of the two invalidation mechanisms from section 3, is suited for, without paying for the added complexity and distributed-reliability risk of an active purge mechanism this product doesn't need.
